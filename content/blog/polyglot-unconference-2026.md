Last October I went to the Polyglot Software Unconference for the first time. I pitched a
talk, half expecting nobody to vote for it, and then watched it show up on the schedule. I
spent the rest of the day in rooms full of people who program in languages I don't, arguing
about the same problems I have. I left thinking it was one of the best days I'd spent with
Vancouver's tech community in a long time, and that more PyLadies should be in those rooms.
(I wrote a full recap, a year late, over on my blog:
[Polyglot Unconference 2025: a recap](https://mariatta.ca/posts/polyglot-unconference-2025-recap/).)

Last year the Polyglot team shared a discount code with PyLadies Vancouver, and this year
they've done it again: **PyLadies Vancouver members get $20 off their ticket**. Thank you to
the [Polyglot Software Association](https://www.polyglotsoftware.com/). They didn't have to,
and it says a lot about the kind of community they're building.

Polyglot 2026 is **Saturday, October 17, 8 AM to 5 PM at UBC Robson Square** (800 Robson

## What Polyglot is, if you haven't been

It's a one-day unconference, which means there is no program in advance. There are no
"speakers" and "attendees," only participants. First thing in the morning, anyone who wants
to lines up and pitches a topic in two or three minutes: a question they're stuck on, a tool
they want to compare notes about, a talk they've prepared. Everyone votes in a web app, the
winning sessions go on the schedule, and then you spend the day moving between four to six
concurrent sessions and talking with the people who showed up for the same thing. It draws a
few hundred people: developers, data folks, designers, PMs, students, professors, founders,
across every language and stack.

If you've ever come home from a conference and said the hallway track was the best part,
Polyglot is a whole day of hallway track.

## A few things I wish I'd known going in

- **You don't have to pitch anything.** Showing up and joining sessions is fully
  participating. If you do pitch, the room is kind about it.
- **But if you have an existing talk, bring it.** Even at an unconference, being prepared
  pays off. I didn't write anything new for Polyglot; I pitched a talk about generating QR
  codes in Python that I'd already given at DjangoCon, and it got voted in. You'd be
  surprised that people actually want to hear it, and if it doesn't get picked, you've lost
  nothing.
- **The sessions aren't all about code.** Some of the ones I got the most out of were about
  teams and leadership. You don't have to be a developer to take part.
- **Moving between sessions is expected.** With no abstracts, an interesting-sounding title
  can turn into a session that isn't for you. The rule is: if you're no longer learning or
  contributing, get up and go to another room. Nobody minds. It took me a while to believe
  this.
- **Bring something you're actually curious about.** The sessions where I had a real
  question beat the ones that just sounded impressive.
- **Tell us that you're going.** I will be there. Come say hi, and if enough of us land in
  the same room, we might pitch a Python session together.
